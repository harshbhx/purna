Purna is a 4-minute daily exercise on SPECS glasses for people who miss the left side after a stroke. Their family sees how it went. Their therapist gets the numbers.

I have a code

After a stroke, half the world can go quiet

About four in ten people who have a stroke on the right side of the brain stop noticing things on their left. They leave the left half of the plate, miss the first words of a line and bump the left door frame. Their eyes work. Their attention doesn't, and they usually don't know it.

It's called left spatial neglect. Therapists train it with paper scanning sheets in the clinic, but in one multicentre Indian study only 7.6% of stroke survivors got any occupational therapy (INSPIRE). Paper also can't see where your head turns. Purna brings the practice to the dining table at home and measures it.

A session at the table

  1. Set it up in ten seconds

    Someone at home puts the glasses on and picks Easy, Standard or Hard. Nothing is shared unless they turn sharing on.

  2. Light the whole ones

    Clay diyas appear across the real table. A pinch lights a whole diya. Chipped ones are left alone.

  3. A hint from the left, never the answer

    Stuck on the right for six seconds? A temple bell rings from the left and a soft beam pulses there. It points to the side, not to a diya, so the searching stays theirs.

  4. See their own search

    A thread replays the order they found the diyas. The ones they missed show where they were.

  5. Send it home

    One pinch sends the result to the family. New glasses show a code like DIYA-7K3P to type in once.

What the family sees

One plain sentence for each session, the left and right sides as two bars, and a line that shows whether the left side is coming back. If three days pass without practice, a gentle reminder on the page. No notifications, no streaks.

Brothers and sisters join with an invite link. Notes from the therapist show up on the same page.

What the therapist gets

A read-only link that lasts seven days and can be stopped at any time. Every session comes with the numbers a cancellation test gives: Centre of Cancellation, asymmetry for the person's left and for each object's left, search order, revisits and head turn.

A CSV for their records, a print layout, and a note box that sends advice back to the family.

Why it's built this way

They see their own search
People with neglect often don't know what they missed. Showing patients where they looked during search improved neglect and self-awareness in a crossover study (Kobe University).
Movement only drifts left
Following motion to the left reduces neglect; motion to the right may make it worse (review). The warm-up never moves anything rightward, and a test checks it.
Some diyas are chipped
Neglect can be about the left side of each object, not only your own left. Cancellation tests use shapes with a gap on one side to tell the two apart (Ota et al.).
The family watches, the therapist decides
Family-led rehabilitation in India didn't reduce death or dependency in the ATTEND trial (Lancet, 2017). Purna gives families visibility and leaves the therapy to the therapist.

Private by design

The glasses send no name, face, camera image or location. The family types whatever name they use at home.

Family keys and doctor links are stored only as one-way fingerprints, so even a copy of the database can't open anyone's page. Doctor links expire, and one button deletes everything for good.
